Form

Four phrases, one seam

The loop is built so bar 16 hands back to bar 1 without a bump. Every fourth bar lands on E major — the raised-third dominant borrowed from A harmonic minor — so the G♯ leans into the A that follows. The seam is the hook, not a splice.

Bars 1–4

Statement

Am F C G — bar 1 is dead on the beat, all four heads struck, so the pulse is stated before anything leans on it. The tune arches A–B–C–B–A–G–E over the tonic, then bar 2 climbs to D6 and falls back.

Bars 5–8

Answer

Am F Dm E — the longest fall in the piece: eight notes from E6 down through the barline into bar 6. Bar 7 turns the line back up to D6, and bar 8 closes on a C leaning down onto G♯.

Bars 9–12

Descent

Am G F E — the climax. A bend lifts the line to E6 in bar 9, then bars 10 and 11 fall five notes and climb five straight back. The hats hand over to a ride cymbal.

Bars 13–16

Turnaround

Dm C F E — brass goes long, bar 15 rocks down and back up through a seven-note V, and a tom fill drops to the crash on bar 1.

Craft

Keeping it off the grid

A sequencer plays exactly what you type, which is how MML ends up sounding like a metronome with opinions. These are the places where the notation is doing the work a player's hands would — and the melody is where it matters most, because a tune that hits every beat head reads as a drill, not a hook.

Swing with no swing command
MML has no shuffle setting, so the bounce is written in: every beat is a dotted eighth and a sixteenth, a8. b16, long-short all the way down. Even eighths sit flat no matter how they are accented.
Straight in front, pushed behind
Beats 1 and 2 sit on the grid in every bar — beat 2 lands on the fifth sixteenth all sixteen times. Beats 3 and 4 are never struck: both are entered early, by a note that starts a sixteenth or an eighth before and holds through. The bar settles, then leans. Bar 1 doesn't lean at all: every beat is struck, which is what makes the other fifteen read as displaced.
Notes that cross the beat
Forty-four percent of the melody starts off the beat and sustains through the next beat head, so the beat is implied rather than hit. Displacing all four beats just sounds unsteady; displacing the back half swings.
Ties over the bar line
>c16&c4 holds one pitch across the barline with no re-attack, so the phrase arrives before the bar does. Nine of the sixteen seams work this way.
One line, not sixteen bars
Three quarters of the melody moves by step, and it will travel seven notes without changing direction — bars 5 and 6 fall eight notes straight through the barline. A tune that changes direction every other note reads as filigree, not melody.
Room to breathe
Hats run in eighths, not sixteenths, and the arpeggio halves to eighths outside the climax. Constant sixteenths across all sixteen bars leave the ear nowhere to rest — the piece dropped from 920 notes to 718 and got easier to listen to.
Ghost snares
N38 ( N38 ) N36 — the parenthesis drops one hit by LV28 and the next restores it. A flam, not a double.
Velocity arc
Each four-bar phrase steps the lead V104 → 112 → 120 → 114, giving the 16 bars a shape instead of a level.
Rotating brass figure
The stab rhythm changes every phrase: backbeat, pushed, downbeat, then sustained. Same notes, four different feels.
Cymbal changeover
Bars 9–12 swap closed hats for a ride and put the arpeggio back into sixteenths, marking the climax with timbre and density rather than volume. It is the only place in the piece running sixteenths.
One bend, saved for the top
Bar 9 is entered with >d8_e4 — hold D6 an eighth, then bend two semitones up into E6. The only bend in the piece, so it still counts as an event.
Gate is articulation
The lead ran at Q8 — a full-length gate, every note touching the next — and the line came out as one smear. At Q6 each note gives back a quarter of its length as silence, and 106 of 107 note pairs are now separated. Same notes, same rhythm; it just reads as played rather than held.

Instrumentation

Two tracks for one kit

An MML track is monophonic, so a drum kit cannot live on a single line — a kick and a crash on the same beat would fight for the channel. The kit is split: J takes kick, snare and toms; K takes hi-hats, and the crashes and ride ride along with them. Chords work the same way — the pad holds two moving voices across C and F while the arpeggio spells out the full triad in sixteenths and the bass covers the root.
